Feeding The Engine

Three numbers describe the cost corpus. Here is what fixing each one actually looks like, in the corpus's own records.

Companion to The Unfed Engine, which ranked the priorities. This page does not argue — it shows the real record before the edit and after it, for every claim in the census below. Every number here was measured; the method note at the foot says how, including the measurement that came back wrong.

Fix one · costs nothing to research

Ten multipliers that cannot move money

These are the cheapest fix on the page and they must go first, because researching a number the engine will throw away is the only genuinely wasted work available here. They split into two kinds, and only one of them is a data problem.

Get-Together / food_style dead · wrong target already researched

The sharpest case in the corpus: a sourced, researched multiplier pointing at rows that do not exist. Hiring a pitmaster adds 40% to the event — grounded to catering-perperson-2026 — and it changes the total by nothing.

The record today 
costFactors: { 'Hire a BBQ caterer / pitmaster': 1.4,
               'Potluck — guests bring sides':  0.75 },
affects: ['p_protein', 'p_sides'],
costFactorProvenance: {
  tier: 'researched', sources: ['catering-perperson-2026'],
  claim: 'Hiring a BBQ pitmaster adds ~40% to total event cost…'
}
What the live food list actually contains 
playbookFoodPlan(Get-Together, 30 guests).list →
[ 'fa-catering', 'p_ice', 'p_fuel', 'p_thermometer',
  'p_tableware', 'p_cleanup' ]
                 ↑ no p_protein. no p_sides.

foodApproach collapses the protein and sides rows into a single fa-* line. The multiplier survived that refactor pointing at the old ids, so it silently no-ops — the failure mode the audit calls unreachable affects. Four of the ten dead values are this decision; two more are Vow Renewal/food_style and Repast/food_source, same cause.

The edit 
affects: ['fa-catering'],

And then measure it, do not assume it. A ×1.4 applied to a collapsed catering line is not obviously the same arithmetic as ×1.4 applied to protein plus sides. _costAudit.test.js already reports whether a factor moves the total and in which direction — the edit is only finished when that row leaves the NO-OP list.

Fish Fry / starch dead · code defect

The other kind, and it is not a data problem at all. This decision points at p_starch, which is in the live list. One of its three multipliers works and two do not:

Same decision, same reachable target, same code path — the multiplier above 1 lands and both below 1 vanish. Low Country Boil/seasoning shows the identical asymmetry (×1.2 works, ×0.9 does not). That is four of the ten, and no amount of pricing research fixes any of them; it is a diagnosis in the cost engine, not in the corpus.

Undiagnosed on purpose. The pattern points at a floor or a rounding step swallowing downward adjustments on a small band, but that has not been read in the code yet — and a cause that has not been read is a guess. Naming it as open is the honest state.

Crab Feast / where_buy  ·  PTA / concessions not a defect

Two of the twelve no-ops are ×1.0. They are supposed to change nothing — a vendor choice that does not move the price is a real answer. Counting them as broken would have inflated the finding by 20%, which is why the table above says ten and not twelve.

Fix two · the actual research programme

The worklist that is already written

This is the finding that should drive the decision. Fifty of fifty-one records state both what the number claims and what evidence would settle it. Nobody has to design an acceptance bar — the corpus set one, fifty times, in advance.

Fish Fry / starch tier: synthesized
Today — a heuristic that says so 
costFactorProvenance: {
  tier: 'synthesized', confidence: 'medium',
  verificationStatus: 'synthesized',
  note:  'Cost factor heuristics need verification against actual pricing.',
  claim: 'Grits cost ~5% less than spaghetti as a fish-fry starch;
          serving both costs ~30% more; fries-only costs ~20% less',
  sufficientWhen: 'Grocery price comparison of grits vs. spaghetti
                   per serving at scale confirms the relative cost
                   differentials'
}

The green line is the whole argument. It is not a wish — it is a runnable instruction: price grits and spaghetti per serving at scale, compare. A researcher needs no brief, no scoping call and no judgement about what would count. Fifty rows carry one of these.

What "done" looks like — and it exists already 
// New Year's Eve Party / toast_pour — the ONE record in finished shape
costFactorProvenance: {
  tier: 'researched', confidence: 'medium',
  verificationStatus: 'cited',
  sources: ['sparklingwine-band-2026', 'sparkling-cider-2026',
            'sparkling-cider-indie-2026'],
  lastVerified: '2026-08-21',
  note:  'Grounded 2026-08-21: sparkling cider at $4.29-8.49 a bottle
          against drinkable sparkling wine at $10-25 confirms the
          cider-at-a-third-to-half ratio the factors encode…',
  claim: 'An all-wine toast costs about 20% more than the split;
          an all-zero-proof toast about halves the toast line…'
}

Same four fields, filled. The transformation is mechanical once the evidence is in hand: tier to researched, verificationStatus to cited, the sources named, the date stamped, and the note rewritten to say what was actually found.

This is not a new process. The purchase surface in the same repository already ran it to completion — 533 cited rows. The template is in-house and finished; it does not need designing.

Fix three · do this before the research, not after

One date in fifty-one

The two money surfaces in this repository hold the same kind of number to two completely different standards. The gap is not quality — it is that one of them has a freshness process and the other has no freshness field.

purchase rowsdecision multipliers

the sequencing argument order matters

Fifty rows researched in one batch, with no lastVerified discipline, produces fifty rows that go stale on the same day — and no way to tell, because there is nothing to read. The purchase surface avoided that by dating every row as it landed. Ship the date field and the census guard first, then research into them.

One record is also bare: PTA / Booster Fundraiser / concessions carries costFactors and no costFactorProvenance at all. It is the only one, and a shape guard would have caught it the day it was written.

The missing guard, in outline 
// the decision-side sibling of moneyProvenanceShape.test.js
every decision with costFactors carries costFactorProvenance   // catches the bare one
every record states a claim AND a sufficientWhen               // 50/51 today
every 'researched' record carries sources[] AND lastVerified   // 1/16 today
the researched/synthesized split is pinned as a number         // so promotion shows up

The last line is the one that repays itself. Pin 16 researched / 34 synthesized and every promotion turns the guard red on purpose, with the reason written beside the new number — the way this repo already records a count that genuinely moved.

Found while photographing the fix · not in the board packet

The date on the screen is older than everything behind it

The shopping hero ends with a freshness stamp: “est. prices Jan 2026”. It is real — correctly formatted from a real constant, not invented. It is also labelling the wrong table.

PRICE_TABLE_META  ·  src/lib/sourcing.js:25 stale label
What the stamp reads 
export const PRICE_TABLE_META = {
  asOf: '2026-01',          // "the vintage of the researched ranges below"
  reviewCadence: 'yearly',
};

“The ranges below” are CANONICAL_PROTEIN_PRICES — ten protein rows in that same file, several cited to 2025 pages. And that table is a fallback: it prices proteins that lack their own sourcingPrices, on non-default sourcing tiers only.

The dollars actually on the hero — Food $195–$515 · supplies $20–$60 — do not come from there. They come from the playbook purchases[].unitCostRange, and those rows carry their own dates:

Measured against the 533 dated rows 
DISPLAYED STAMP      : asOf = 2026-01   ("est. prices Jan 2026")
dated purchase rows  : 533
  earliest           : 2026-08-14
  latest             : 2026-09-17
  by month           : { '2026-08': 529, '2026-09': 4 }
  OLDER than the stamp: 0

Not one priced row on that screen is as old as the date the screen displays. The stamp under-claims by seven to eight months — the safe direction to be wrong in, and still wrong. A host reading “Jan 2026” in September has been handed a reason to distrust numbers that were re-verified last month.

The fix, in the house rule 
- const PRICE_VINTAGE = format(PRICE_TABLE_META.asOf)   // one hardcoded constant
+ derive the vintage from the rows this plan actually rendered
  — single source of truth, no hardcoding, and it cannot drift again
    because the label and the data become the same fact.

This is a fourth money surface — sourcing.js, alongside the purchase rows, the decision multipliers and the geography note. The board packet counted three. Found by photographing the density fix, which is the argument for driving a change in a browser rather than reading the diff.

Shipped 24 Sep · and two things this page got wrong

The price is kept now — and the fix was not where I said

The sections above frame the corpus as needing a research programme. Tracing the code found something better: the app already pulls real shelf prices on request, and throws every one away.

kroger.py  ·  search-list shipped

A host taps “Price this list at a store near you”, picks a store, and the backend returns a real shelf price per line. The host saw it. Nothing persisted it. Every one of those prices was evidence about a corpus row, generated at zero marginal cost against an API budget already provisioned, and discarded at the end of the request.

What a priced line becomes now 
p_ice @ store 01400376, Sep 2026  →  $2.99 (7 lb, UNIT)

kind:      pricing
assetId:   Get-Together
fieldPath: p_ice.unitCostRange   ← the field a KCR would target

Attributable, or not kept. An observation is recorded only when the line names its corpus row and the request names the playbook. Matching a free-text product name back to p_ice by string would be the same guess geoItemMap refuses to make. Both fields are optional on the wire — without them the list prices exactly as before and records nothing.

Idempotent per (row, store, month). Two hosts pricing ice at the same store this month saw one shelf; counting it twice would manufacture the corroboration a later “N observations agree” rule depends on. A different store or month is a genuinely independent look.

Server-side, and not by preference: kas.py gates every write behind require_admin, so a browser writing an observation would fail for every real host — the only people who generate price data. No event id, no identity, no ZIP travels; a store id is a shop, not a person.

two recommendations, disproved by testing them I was wrong

1. “bake:knowledge is a hand-crank that will break — put it in the release chain.” Tested by publishing a KCR without re-baking:

x snapshot is STALE - run: npm run bake:knowledge

The gate already catches it, in verify:push and twice in CI. The snapshot is source-controlled on purpose, so what ships is what was reviewed. Baking during release would have the deploy regenerate the artifact instead — strictly worse. Change dropped.

2. “The backend isn’t updating consistently.” Largely wrong. BLS caches per (region, month) at a 6-hour success TTL — at most sixteen upstream calls a day across all hosts, ever. Kroger is on-request by design; a scheduled scrape would spend a 450-list/day ceiling on nobody’s event. And the fallbacks are already proven: threeLayersOfPrice is 30 green unit tests plus 8 browser cases covering no keys, no store nearby, and an unconfigured build.

What was actually missing was observability, not correctness. So /kroger/status now answers two questions rather than one:

{ "configured": true,   // can we price a list?
  "recording":  true    // can we KEEP it? — needs a database, which pricing does not }

A deployment pricing lists perfectly while silently recording nothing is invisible from the host’s side and from the response. One flag would have hidden exactly the gap worth seeing.

Update, 24 Sep. Step 3 has a second source now, and it is the better one: real shelf prices from hosts pricing their own lists, kept as observations (section above). It costs no API budget, prices the items people actually buy, and arrives with a store and a date attached. The sufficientWhen worklist stays — several of those sentences literally ask for “current shelf prices at the same store,” which is what an observation is.

Not the board's call: whether NGW maintains a priced corpus or consumes a pricing feed. Fetching does not remove this work — a fetched price still needs a claim and a date, or it is an unlabelled number with better latency. That is a product and cost commitment, and it belongs to the owner.

How every number here was measured. Effectiveness (118 / 106 / 10) from src/lib/playbooks/_costAudit.test.js, a pre-existing report not written for this page. The provenance census and the purchase contrast from a temporary pass over ALL_PLAYBOOKS, reading d.costFactors, d.costFactorProvenance and pb.purchases[].costProvenance. Records quoted verbatim from src/lib/playbooks/data/.

And one measurement came back wrong. The census first read d.costProvenance — the purchase-row key — and reported 51 of 51 decisions with no provenance at all. Dramatic, quotable, false: the decision key is costFactorProvenance. Recorded rather than deleted, because two key names for one idea is itself a finding, and the next reader will reach for the same wrong one.
