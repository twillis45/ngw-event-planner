Decision Layer Remediation

The engines compute. The decision layer annotates. Closing the gap between what the app says it derives and what it actually derives — sequenced by measured field coverage, not by guess. All four phases shipped, and the audit queue behind them is now worked to the end of what is ours. Three of the last four findings moved no number at all — the measurement was the deliverable, and the fix was somebody else’s call. A second pass on 23 September closed six of the seven audit rows still open, four of which turned out to have been fixed already and never re-measured. Then the four open board calls were decided and shipped — and two of them reversed work published hours earlier the same day. What remains is one row, and it waits on a retirement decision rather than on work.

A correction to this page

The fifth publication reported ask coverage as “267/267 decisions · 594/594 rows · 100%”. Re-measured today across all 45 playbooks: 121 of 260 decisions author an ask (47%), and at a T-31d horizon 267 of 357 board rows carry one (75%).

Both numbers can be true of different things, and that is the trap. Every row can produce a question — the board falls back to authoredQuestion(d.label) when no ask is authored, so no row is ever mute. Every decision authors its own is a different claim, and it is not true. 100% was the fallback working, not the corpus being complete.

And the 47% was right in its arithmetic and wrong in its conclusion. Measured again since: 138 of those 139 unauthored decisions have a question-shaped label, and the fallback returns the label itself — so the board already asks the host something, and it reads fine (“Seated dinner or buffet / family-style?”, “Who handles the food?”). Landing 139 drafted strings over a corpus that is already authored would have been churn. What is actually thin is the tail: median label-question is five words, and thirteen are three or fewer against option lists they cannot cover — “Alcohol?” over four options. Twelve now author a real question; the thirteenth was left because “Which sport?” is already a fine question.

Second pass — 23 September

The audit table below was carried forward for four days without being re-run against the tree. Checked row by row this evening, four of its seven open rows had already been fixed and one was wrong in its count. That is the same defect the page is about, committed by the page: a fact owned by one accessor, restated by the reader beside it without asking. A stale finding asserted as current is its own kind of invented data.

The food sheet said the ice was free. hostv2 rendered the per-unit rate through its whole-dollar fmt — right for a line total, ruinous for a rate, because most of this corpus’s rates are under a dollar. Counted across all 440 per-unit lines: 84 had a bound round to $0, 22 collapsed to “$0–$0” outright, 142 had a sub-dollar low bound and so printed “$0” or “$1” — a number the plan never authored — and 186 were distorted by more than 5%.

The data was never wrong: the engine stores these to the cent. Only the display threw the cents away. And the rule was not new — the legacy CRA food sheet had worked it out inline at its own call site, and hostv2 was built beside it and reached for the generic helper. One definition now, imported by both.

A shoe insert was one guard away from being priced as flatbread. The match-quality guard was written for the line-total function and lived only there, so it decided the total and nothing else — the price a host reads was rendered from the same match without ever asking. Probed against a live Baltimore store:

“injera”        →  Airplus® Gel Women’s Orthotic Insole Shoe Inserts  $8.99
“espresso cups” →  Private Selection® Espresso Roast Coffee Pods     $6.99
“prosecco”      →  Tuscany Candle™ Peach Prosecco Wax Melts        $3.29

Only the last carries a word a blocklist can see. So there are two fixes and the second matters more: the gate moved to the one place that decides whether a line has a store price, and the store layer now prints what it priced. A heuristic catches a familiar way of being wrong, never every way — a host reading the product name catches all of them.

And the search term stopped being the multiply licence. Sending a line’s display text as the query — what a line with no curated term gets — matched nothing for eleven of twelve non-allowlisted lines probed: trash bags, charcoal, disposable cups, napkins, bread, buns. Eleven live-probed terms were added that buy a shelf reference and can never produce a total. The multiply allowlist is saturated at 41: all 444 corpus lines swept, 94 carry a mass or volume unit, and the other 53 are baskets, “ingredients” lines, or entries already removed on live evidence.

The food factor reported today’s date as its data month. BLS publishes Average Price with a few weeks’ lag, so a September request routinely returns July’s figure — and the endpoint read the value off the data point and threw the point’s own date away one line later, then stamped date.today() on the result. It now reads the point’s own year and period, refuses M13 (the annual average, which is not a month), and when the series disagree reports the oldest: the factor is a ratio across all of them, so it is only as current as its stalest input.

Same file, same cache: only successes were stored, so during a BLS outage every request paid the full 20-second timeout to be told the same thing. Failures are now cached on a five-minute leash against six hours for a success — an outage ends, and a stale “unavailable” must not outlive it.

A detector that could not fire, for five days. timingConflict was written on 18 September so a contradiction would stop being filed as an absence, and it reached nobody — because the board drops the field it reads. The row carries the derived due date and days-out; when was gone before any caller could ask, so every call returned null and every reader took that as “no conflict.” A correct detector pointed at a population that had already lost the field it needs.

Three decisions tell a host to start later than a real dated source supports — Day Party and Retirement Party venues at T-28d and T-35d against a two-month floor, and Surprise Proposal’s hidden photographer at T-30d. The audit recorded four. Holiday Party’s venue has since been authored to T-75d, inside the window, and is correctly no longer one. No authored deadline moves; what changed is that the disagreement is now a sentence on the decision card.

The rings had a pack-it date and no order date, and the brief that found it was wrong in an instructive way. Elopement’s rings row has read “Order/size rings weeks ahead” since it was written; its buyAt: T-3d is a PACK-IT date, so a $100–$2,000 essential with a six-week production queue looked like a three-day errand — on an event people travel to, where the rings must be in hand before the trip.

The brief said three split-act rows needed a second row authored. Swept across all 45 playbooks, eight rows name a second act: five already put buyAt at the stated lead (one date, one act — the corpus’s convention); two are already covered by checklist tasks at exactly the leads their own prose states; one was genuinely uncovered; and one is conditional with no researched number.

A whole mechanism was built and reverted on that measurement — a purchase field with its own task emitter, sixteen green tests, money proved not to move. Two things killed it: it would have duplicated the two tasks that already exist, and it fed an engine reader with no consumer outside the frozen CRA shell. Building on a surface the host does not read is the same defect as not building at all.

So the fix is a task at T-56d, and its lead is researched rather than chosen: two independent jewellers, one UK and one US, both read in full, converging on 6–8 weeks and giving the same mechanism. Sweeping the new timing category over all 260 decisions found both things a regex hides — a gain (a real ring-sourcing decision now cites two dated sources where it cited none) and a false positive caught before it shipped (a “lock the ceremony moment (vows, readings, rings/keepsake…)” row that would have reported a false contradiction against a jeweller’s floor).

The safety lane, in full

Asked which of two dietary-collection mechanisms is better for a host’s guests, the answer turned out to be neither of the two being compared. A dietary fact can live in three places. The spread’s allergen flagging read two of them.

Crab Feast · tap “Nut allergy” and “Shellfish”, then read the shopping list

store specialDiets dietaryResolved items flagged
nothing recorded — false none
decision CHIPS — false NONE
dietCounts (the drill-in) 2 diets false Blue crabs, Steamed shrimp
per-guest roster — true Blue crabs, Steamed shrimp

The chips are the picker most hosts actually touch. Their output was byte-identical to recording nothing — while the board counted the decision settled and the toast said so. On the decision whose own why reads: “One guest with an allergy needs a separate plate kept away from the crab steam and the tools.”

A correction to the lane above it. Making those chips multi-select fixed what they RECORDED and what they DISPLAYED. It did not make one spread item safer, because the store they write to had no reader in the flagging engine. Phase 04 was reported as a safety fix; this is the part that earns the name.

And the marker was invisible where it landed. Only the first two tags on a spread row render; the rest fold into “+N”. The diet flag sat fourth, behind decision open, essential and day-of — so on Blue crabs, a row carrying the first two, the allergen marker was always the one collapsed. Driven at 390px: the row read decision open · essential · +1. It goes first now. Every other tag on that row is about shopping; this is the only one whose absence can hurt somebody.

The tiering was preserved, not flattened. Chips join as a flag source only — never specialDiets, which carries counts they cannot know, and never dietaryResolved, which means resolved per guest and only the roster earns. A chip says which restrictions exist at this table. It does not claim how many guests have them, or that anyone was asked by name.

That efficiency number is what ruled out the cheap fixes rather than a guess. fullyParallel is unset so tests inside one file run serially — but with no idle worker slots there is nothing to reclaim. The sleeps total only ~3–4 min across the whole matrix; the expectation was higher and it was wrong. And the job was 3 minutes from its own timeout — 27.0 against timeout-minutes: 30, a 10% margin on a repo that auto-deploys from main.

workers: 2 is unchanged. Three workers on the 4-vCPU runner would have been free, and would have reversed a deliberate fix: two runs at the same SHA once produced disjoint failure sets. Each shard runs two workers on its own machine, so the flake posture is untouched. That is the whole appeal.

The fix’s own first run caught the next defect. Sharding worked; the merge job printed no total — its output stopped at test 611 of 1,216 with no summary line. --reporter=list streams for a terminal and loses its output when stdout is a pipe. And measured separately: playwright test on a failing spec exits 1, while merge-reports on that same blob exits 0. A merge step can never be the verdict — it paints a red matrix green.

The four board calls, decided

Everything with a mechanical answer had shipped; these four needed a ruling, a pricing judgement or an authoring decision. All four were taken on 23 September under the owner’s standing delegation. Two of them reversed work published hours earlier the same day, which is recorded here rather than smoothed over.

#1 — RANK THE CLOSING WINDOW. The 2026-08-17 bar is met. Its own re-derived case had not inverted for five weeks: a certificate 27 days dead scored 4.90, and a vendor reconfirm whose window shuts in three days scored 0.00, because that raise emitted no consequence signal at all and latenessBoost pays only for being PAST a window.

The packet’s objection was right, and is answered rather than overruled. It warned that the obvious predicate is a DATE window, not a closing-window predicate — it “does not know the difference between ‘this cannot be done later’ and ‘this happens to be due soon’” — and proved it by turning its own negative control red. So the term is paid on a DECLARED closing window. A raise knows whether its window reopens; a scorer cannot see that from two dates.

Reconfirm Ironwood 0.00 -> 5.50 declares the window
Ask Ironwood about insurance 4.90 -> 4.90 untouched
Buy day-of emergency kit 0.00 -> 0.00 due TODAY, declares nothing
synthetic scheduled gate 4.00 -> 4.00 declares nothing

The last two rows are the objection, answered by measurement. Sized at 3.5, below the lateness floor of 4, so a genuinely late item still leads a bare closing window. No lateness constant moved; all four ruled guards pass unchanged. The recorder written to fail the moment anyone implemented this did — which is how it was forced through the packet rather than past it.

#3 — REFUSE OR FLOOR: NEITHER. belowRequiredVendors had sat on the estimate result since 19 September with no reader, so a host reached a one-tap “Use $300” on a Surprise Proposal whose own roster requires a photographer and a ring — a $1,750 floor.

Refusing the headline costs them the one number they came for. Flooring it builds their primary figure out of 224 vendor ranges carrying zero provenance. So they get both, and are told which is which — the floor only ever as “start around”, because a range’s bottom is the only honest reading of a band with no sources. The headline is not moved, and a test asserts that, since quietly flooring it is the failure this call turned on.

Where it fires, measured: 315 type-and-guest-count combinations swept, 15 fire, and every one is a small headcount — a 10-person wedding, a 20-person conference, a 2-person proposal. Per-head scales with guests and a vendor floor does not, so the gap lives at the bottom of the range and closes as the event grows.

#5 — THE THREE LATE DEADLINES WERE FALSE, AND THE WARNING FOR THEM HAD ALREADY SHIPPED. timingDisagreementNote reached the decision card that afternoon. The first thing it did on a real board was tell a Day Party host that “Where (daytime outdoor)” was four weeks out against a two-month booking floor.

Its options are Backyard / Rooftop / Patio / Rented outdoor space. Three of four require no booking at all. Reading the three flagged decisions instead of counting them, all three were the same mistake — including Surprise Proposal’s “Photographer hidden or known to your partner?”, which decides what the photographer pretends to be, not whether to hire one.

That is the exact failure timingProvenance.js’s own header warns about, committed by that file: the lead-window gate caught the GROUNDING case and let the CONTRADICTION case through, because a contradiction is what you get when the window rejects a match the pattern should never have made. Four days as a finding on this page, and a few hours in front of hosts.

The veto is structural, not a word: a decision offering somewhere the host already has is a setting choice — unless its label says it is a booking, which is how Wedding’s “Venue + date (book FIRST)” keeps its grounding. That exemption came from listing what the first version actually removed instead of trusting it. Matched 33 → 28, grounded 28 → 26, late conflicts 3 → 0. No authored date moved in either direction.

#4 — TWO DATE PREMIUMS RUNNING 3–16× THE ONLY PUBLISHED MEASUREMENT. A 100-guest wedding on a June Saturday estimated 35% above the same wedding on a January Wednesday. Two factors produced all of it, and this repo had already measured both.

Saturday’s +20% is now a flag with no multiplier. The survey measuring whole-event spend — the matching unit — puts Saturday 1.2% over Sunday, and Saturday is not even the dearest day in it. The rate card that says 63% prices one venue’s rental in one market. Picking something between 1.2% and 63% would have been a third figure no source states, which is what the old one already was. Lessons worth keeping

The right detector on the wrong population is still a wrong answer — this happened five times in two days. A region probe on a playbook with no region items. A ladder probe reading the structure the ranker does not use. A containment probe testing the wrong inequality, returning a confident zero violations over eight real ones. A discriminator nearly wired on a premise wrong for 14 of 45 playbooks. And isCircularAsk run over the deliberate label-as-question path, flagging 138 rows that were fine. Every one produced a number that looked like evidence.

So a throwaway probe needs the same premise test as a shipped guard. Before believing a negative, make the probe report a case already known to be positive. A probe that cannot find the known defect has not measured the corpus — it has measured itself. That is now written beside the timings, because it cost more than every slow command combined.

A guard that cannot fail is worse than none, and two were caught here. An e2e guard written for the ladder fix passed with the fix reverted — it was reading a count the shell does not use, so it was deleted and the commit says why. A flat three-day tolerance in the deadline sweep swallowed the very Birthday cake it was written for; rounding error scales with the quantity, so the slack had to be proportional.

Recording is a result. Three findings here were real and none was ours to fix — a board-ruled scoreboard, eight authored price bands, a per-head model on flat-cost events. A commit whose first line is “NO NUMBER MOVED”, with the measurement behind it and a test that fails the day someone changes it quietly, converts an open question into one decision.

A green suite covered a surface that had not moved — three times in one day. The dietary fold that closed over its own answer, the chip store that reached no reader, the allergen marker sitting fourth in a list that renders two. Every one was found by driving the built app in Chromium. None was found by a test.

A test that pins a behaviour by list position is pinning the ranking too — whether or not it means to. Four did, and all four passed for months over a boundary where the board’s entire score was zero.

A gate that fires on the wrong line is nearly as bad as one that does not fire. The first version of the label-coupling guard failed three tests when one label was reworded, and only one of them was the news. It now fails exactly one — the map — pointing straight at the finding.

A detector is not a fix until something can call it. timingConflict was correct, tested and unreachable for five days, because the layer between it and its caller had already dropped the field it reads. The premise test that asserts the field is absent on every board row is the one that names it — and it is the test the original work should have written first.

Measure the gap before building the mechanism. A correct, tested, money-safe feature was built for a gap that two authored tasks already filled, and reverted the same session. It also fed an engine reader with no consumer on the shell people use. Building on a surface the host does not read is the same defect as not building at all.

Re-measure a carried-forward finding before asserting it. Four of seven open audit rows were already fixed, and one was wrong in its count. This page asserted all seven for four days. A stale finding restated as current is its own kind of invented data — and the page that exists to catch that class committed it.

Sweep a new pattern over the whole corpus before trusting it. One new timing category produced a real grounding gain and a false contradiction on a ceremony decision, and neither was visible from reading the regex. Saturation is not automatically a defect either — the aging cap is real, the board does not visibly freeze, and recording that was the result.

Declare, don’t infer. The closing-window axis was refused as a date predicate and shipped as a declared one, because a raise knows whether its window reopens and the scorer cannot see that from two dates. The date version’s only evidence that it was wrong was a negative control going red — the same principle the whole programme has been applying, now paid for by the scorer rather than re-derived beside it.

A behaviour lock going red is the system working. theRulingsOwnBarIsUnmet was written in September to fail the moment anyone implemented the missing axis, “so whoever adds it is forced through the packet rather than past it.” It did exactly that. The file now records the decision and keeps every negative control it had, because those are what prove the fix did not buy one direction by selling the other.

When both options are wrong, publish both numbers. The estimate-shortfall call put refusing the headline against raising it to the vendor floor. Refusing costs the host the one number they came for; flooring launders 224 unsourced vendor ranges into the most load-bearing figure on the screen. The host gets both and is told which is which — strictly more information than either option, and the answer this codebase reaches everywhere else.
