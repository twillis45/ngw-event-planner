# Bands that their own commodity evidence does not support

**Date:** 2026-08-18 · Found while sourcing, not while looking for it
**Status:** measured and recorded. NOT fixed — changing a band is a product call.

## What this is

Grounding a priced row means checking that the cited figures actually bracket its
`unitCostRange`. Doing that across ~40 rows turned up **twenty-six**, of which **ten have since been retired**, whose band the
evidence does not support. They are listed here rather than papered over with a
citation, because a source that does not support the number is worse than no
source: it makes an unverified figure look checked.

## RETIRED — ten findings were mine, not the corpus's

**Three more resolved by pricing the RAW cut instead of the restaurant's output.**

`juneteenthCookout:88` (whole packer, $5–10/lb) and `graduation:66` (BBQ spread,
$4–10/lb) were both recorded against **smoked BBQ sold by the pound at
$17.99–34.49** — that is a restaurant's finished product. A whole packer is
bought **raw at $3.00–5.99/lb** and the host smokes it. Same word, same animal,
three to seven times apart.

`juneteenthCookout:97` (strawberries, $1–3/lb) was recorded against $2.60–3.00/lb
retail. USDA advertised pricing puts a **2lb package at $2.55/lb**, inside the
band.



**`lowCountryBoil:83` table paper, $0.10–0.50/ft — RESOLVED, band is correct.**

Recorded against 40lb bleached-white butcher paper in 700–1,000ft foodservice
rolls at $0.054–0.073 a foot. A host does not buy those. **Consumer
natural-brown kraft is $0.24–0.25 a linear foot** — four times the foodservice
grade and squarely inside the band. The same error shape as the seasoning tin
and the multipack soda: I measured the wrong tier.



**`crabFeast:205` Old Bay / J.O. seasoning, $4–9/lb — RESOLVED, band is correct.**

Recorded here against a 6oz supermarket tin at $15.97/lb. That is not the size a
crab feast buys. At the sizes it does: Old Bay 24oz is **$6.67–9.30/lb**, a
7.5lb jug **$5.27/lb**, and J.O. No. 2 in a 5lb container **$4.30/lb**. The band
brackets those exactly.

The small-container premium is 1.9–3.1× across every spice measured, which is
the whole reason a per-pound band written from a tin looks wrong.



**Four drinks rows and one buffet row have been RESOLVED. Their bands were
right; my comparison was wrong.**

The drinks findings all rested on MULTIPACK arithmetic — soda at $0.25–0.60 a
can from a 12-pack, water at $0.13–0.25 from a 24-pack, coffee at $0.10–0.30
home-brewed. Wave-three research priced the formats those rows actually buy:

| format | figure |
|---|---|
| single 20oz soda bought on its own | **$2.29–3.39** |
| ready-to-drink juice, per 8oz serving | **$0.69–0.88** |
| 96oz catering coffee traveller, per cup | **$1.67–2.08** |
| canned mocktail, per 8oz can | **$4.88–14.75** |

Against those, `birthday:69`, `graduation:69`, `repast:74` and `teamRetreat:82`
are all correct and are now cited. `anniversary:98` and `vowRenewal:111` are
cited to the same evidence.

**This is why a mismatch is recorded rather than acted on.** Had I "corrected"
those bands when I found them, I would have edited four correct numbers to match
a price for a product the host was never buying.

## RETIRED — one finding was mine, not the corpus's

**`holidayParty:85` carved turkey or ham (buffet trays), $6–16/lb — RESOLVED, band is correct.**

It was recorded here against raw spiral ham at $2.25/lb. That was the wrong
comparison: the row buys a PREPARED tray. Deli-counter cooked sliced roast beef
is **$14.99/lb** and smoked turkey breast by the pound is **$19.99/lb**, which
brackets the band exactly. The row is now cited to those figures.

This is what the list is for. A recorded mismatch is a question, not a verdict —
and the third wave of research answered this one by finding the channel the band
was pricing all along. Two more rows below may resolve the same way.

## The pattern

The two directions are not random and they do not cancel out.

**Drinks and prepared items read high.** The likeliest explanation is a purchase
CHANNEL no source covers: a single-serve bottle rather than a 12-pack, a catered
tray rather than raw meat. If so the bands are right and the sources are simply
pricing a different thing — but nothing in the corpus says so, so a reader cannot
tell that from an over-estimate.

**Snacks and seasonings read low.** Chips are a genuinely expensive commodity by
weight. Seasonings are worse: Old Bay is bought in a 6oz tin at $5.99, which
*feels* cheap, but per pound it is $15.97 — four times the row's ceiling. Even
a 50lb foodservice case of crab boil is $6.70/lb, above the crawfish row's
entire band. A per-pound band written from the feel of a small tin will be wrong
by roughly the ratio of the tin to a pound.

That split — the same author estimating one category high and another low — is
what makes this worth surfacing rather than silently correcting.

## What was NOT done, deliberately

- **No band was changed.** These numbers drive host budgets. Moving them is a
  product decision with an event pro in the room, not a side effect of a
  sourcing pass.
- **No citation was attached to any row above.** Citing sources that contradict
  the band would convert an honest estimate into a false claim, which is the one
  outcome worse than leaving it unsourced.
- Where a row was groundable with an honest caveat, it WAS grounded and the
  caveat is in the claim — `graduation:67` says its ceiling holds only because
  cheap components outweigh the chips by volume, and the Kwanzaa corn row says
  its band is a December price against summer sources.

## The ask

Each row needs one of two answers, and only a person who knows the product can
give it:

1. **The band is right** — it prices a channel (single-serve, prepared, catered)
   the sources do not cover. Then the row needs a source for THAT channel, and
   the claim should name it.
2. **The band is wrong** — it is an estimate that shelf prices now contradict.
   Then it should move, and the sourcing above is what it should move to.
