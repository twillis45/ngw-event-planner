# Model D vs. the leaders — five-fleet competitive read

Date: August 19, 2026 · Five parallel research agents, all facts web-verified
same day with cited URLs (full reports in session transcript; key facts inline).
Question: where does the Model D paywall (ruled 2026-08-18, three sittings)
stand against how the market actually monetizes?

Model D recap: free = first local single-day event, complete, + one full
vendor-brief loop. $39 One-Event Pass, one SKU, three doors: (1) briefs beyond
the first vendor, (2) destination/multi-day, (3) any event after the first.

---

## Verdict up front

**Two of the three doors stand on strong, named precedent. One is novel and
needs a framing adjustment, not a removal. The $39 price point is uncannily
well-precedented — three competitors charge one-time prices within $10 of it.
And Model D's four trust conditions independently converged on exactly the
practices the research says separate accepted gates from resented ones.**

---

## Door 3 — pay per additional event: STRONGEST

- **Evite Premium is literally this model at mainstream scale**: one-time
  per-event packages, $17.99–$99.99 tiered by guest count. Reputationally
  clean — grumbling about price, never accusations of deception.
- The consumer per-instance band for a bounded, episodic job clusters at
  **$15–$100**: FreeTaxUSA $15.99/state return, TurboTax $79–$159/return,
  Paperless Post ~$46–57 per 100-guest premium send, WedSites $99–$199
  one-time, Joy add-on bundles $99–$299.
- **WedSites sells a $39 one-time tier. Joy's premium website tier is a $39
  one-time. Appy Couple is $49 one-time.** Three shipping products within
  $10 of our price, same buyer, same one-time structure.
- The counter-example proves the point: RSVPify forces subscriptions on
  one-time hosts and its reviews say exactly why that fails ("expensive for
  one-time events").
- Professional planner SaaS (HoneyBook $29–109/mo, Aisle Planner $69–229/mo,
  Planning Pod, Dubsado, 17hats) has **zero** per-event pricing — the
  one-event slot is structurally empty on the pro side, exactly as D-2
  predicted when it parked Planner Pro.

## Door 1 — briefs beyond the first vendor: STRONG, with one honest caveat

- Communication/sharing gates are established and successful when done
  right: Figma (view free / edit paid), Calendly (one live event type),
  Notion (10 guests), **DocuSign envelopes — the nearest structural analog
  to a vendor-brief gate** (free: 3 sends; paid tiers by envelope volume).
- DocuSign proves the gate monetizes AND shows precisely what breeds
  resentment: caps discovered on collision, monthly-looking prices hiding
  annual caps, $1.50–5 per-unit overages. An entire competitor category
  markets "no envelope limits" against it.
- **The research's four accepted-practice rules, derived independently,
  match Model D's ruled conditions almost verbatim:**
  1. Disclose the cap at signup and at the counter, not at the wall →
     Model D: disclosure at event creation.
  2. Gate creation/sending, never reading → Model D: gate fires only at
     generating a new vendor's brief, never on a shared one.
  3. Free allowance completes one genuine small use → Model D: one full
     brief loop (brief → vendor confirms → record updates).
  4. Flat unlock over per-unit overage → Model D: $39 unlocks the whole
     event, no per-brief metering.
- **The caveat:** couple-facing wedding platforms (Knot, Zola, WeddingWire,
  Joy) never gate vendor communication — but for a structural reason that
  does not apply to us: vendor inquiries ARE their revenue (vendors pay for
  the leads). We sell no vendor leads, so gating the brief does not eat our
  own flywheel. Worth stating plainly in any future positioning: we charge
  the host because we do not charge the vendor — no pay-to-play placement,
  no lead fees inflating vendor quotes. That is a differentiator, not a
  liability, but only if said out loud.
- The Punchbowl lesson (the category's worst backlash: "free" marketing
  with no send possible without a card) is the failure Model D's free-loop
  design already avoids.

## Door 2 — destination/multi-day: NOVEL — keep it, reframe it

- **No competitor gates on trip complexity.** Hotel blocks and travel pages
  are universally free-with-hotel-commission (Joy headlines free room
  blocks as acquisition; Knot/Engine, Kleinfeld, HotelPlanner all
  hotel-paid). Wanderlog's free tier includes multi-day itineraries; its
  $39.99/yr Pro sells convenience, not trip type.
- **But paid destination-oriented planning software exists at exactly our
  price**: Appy Couple, $49 one-time, is specifically recommended by 2026
  roundups FOR destination weddings because of its travel modules. TripProf
  sells an organizer-pays per-trip unlock. "One organizer pays, guests
  never do" is established.
- **The adjustment (copy, not mechanics):** sell the pass as the whole
  premium product with the destination/multi-day toolkit as its headline
  feature — the Appy Couple framing — rather than naming "destination" as
  the tripwire. The gate can still key on `isDestination`/`spanIntel()`
  (that is when the toolkit becomes relevant), but the pass sheet and the
  creation-time disclosure should say "the pass includes the full
  destination toolkit," not "destination events cost money."
- **One future landmine to pre-commit on:** if a hotel-block/booking layer
  is ever added, it must be free-with-commission like the entire market —
  the pass gates planning software, never the booking of rooms. Hosts are
  trained hard to expect that layer free.

## The free tier vs. category norms

- In couple-facing wedding tech the host pays for nothing functional — the
  products are funded by vendors, registries, and hotels. We cannot copy
  that (no marketplace, no registry commerce, no commission deals yet), so
  a host-paid model is structurally forced — and the research shows the
  accepted host-paid shapes are exactly ours: one-time per event, or
  design/premium tiers.
- The only things couples pay for anywhere: custom domains ($15–40), print
  stationery, design subscriptions ($12–39/mo), card-fee pass-throughs.
  Notably: **a custom domain is the one universally-accepted paid feature**
  — a cheap future add-on candidate that fights no norm.
- Partiful (free-everything, fees only on ticket transactions) is the
  Gen-Z-consumer path we are NOT taking — right call for the milestone-host
  buyer, who runs one high-stakes event, not a party cadence.

## Recommended amendments to the ruling (copy-level, no mechanics change)

1. **Pass-sheet framing:** lead with what the pass IS (the whole premium
   product: unlimited vendor sharing + the destination/multi-day toolkit +
   your future events), not which events "cost money." Appy Couple model.
2. **Say the flywheel flip out loud** somewhere a host reads: vendors pay
   nothing here — no lead fees, no pay-to-play placement — which is why the
   pass exists. It converts Door 1's category-norm deviation into a trust
   claim.
3. **Pre-commit:** any future hotel-block/booking layer ships
   free-with-commission, outside the pass. Record it now so a revenue
   conversation later does not relitigate it against a host expectation the
   whole market has set.

None of these touch the ruled mechanics, the three doors, the grandfather
rule, or the price. The board's Model D survives contact with the market;
the only genuinely novel bet (Door 2) has a $49 one-time neighbor proving
hosts pay for destination-oriented planning software when it is framed as
the product, not the penalty.
